You see prediction markets and sports books everywhere right now, and today we're explaining how the two are different. Take a football game between a red team versus a blue team, where a sports book works like a vending machine. Its data scientists figure out red's chances, say it's 40%, and turn that into a sticker price. Baked into that is a little extra margin called the vig here in black, and the price moves as news breaks, but the vig will always be there. Then you either buy at that price or you don't, but you can't negotiate with the vending machine. Every bet you place on a book, you're placing against the house, so the book makes money when its customers, all added up, lose more than they win, and that incentive shapes the business. So, mispricing parlays to expand the house's cut, targeting customers who tend to lose a lot, and limiting or banning bettors who win too consistently. Over on the prediction market or exchange side, there's no vending machine, and it's more like the stock market with buyers and sellers constantly trading. Here, you buy a contract that says, for example, the red team will win, and if they do, it pays a dollar. If not, zero. That contract can cost anywhere on this line from zero to one dollar, and the same 40% chances we talked about before will cost 40 cents, simple as that. Nobody at the exchange picked that price, though, as it comes from those people trading those contracts. And when you buy red, someone on the other end is taking blue. If we go a layer deeper, here's what that looks like in practice with a real order book from a random football game last week, which sits right here at 48% chance that one team wins. The axis here represents the price of the contracts, and there's a row for every single cent from one up to 99. The green bars down here are the buyers, and the length of each bar shows how many contracts people are willing to buy at that price. And then the red bars up top are the sellers. And again, the size of each bar shows how many contracts willing to be sold at that price. If we zoom in right at the middle, where the highest buy price means the lowest sell price, a trade happens, and that last trade is the price you see on your screen. No company is deciding, it's just the exchange matching the two sides, and the price moves up and down wherever buyers and sellers meet over time. Again, you can think of it like the stock market where Apple isn't the one setting the price of the Apple stock, but rather buyers and sellers pushing and pulling it back and forth like a game of tug-of-war. And because they're constantly competing, the gap between them keeps shrinking, essentially the opposite of a sports book pushing to increase margins. All of this leads to two very different business models. A sports book earns when customers lose and their revenue equals the average of customer losses. An exchange on the other hand earns a fee every time someone trades, win or lose, optimizing for volume alone. Those fees are usually published and transparent and look like this, so there isn't a question as to what the exchange keeps, unlike the sports book. So, the apps on your phone may look alike, but the structure underneath isn't. One is a house and the other is a market and that difference shapes everything about how each one makes money and what that means for the customer.
