So if I had to summarize the first half of 2026, it would be very simple. Stagflation concerns. When we came into 2026, already around the world, inflation was running above target and central banks were reconsidering the idea of further interest rate cuts. After February 28th, we have added to that additional concerns about higher commodity prices and supply chain disruptions which could threaten to put inflation even higher. >> When we think about stagflation, it's important to consider the two portions of that. The classic definition of stagflation back in the 1970s was high inflation and high unemployment. Today we don't have runaway inflation like we had back in the 70s, but we certainly have inflation above trend. So we have that flation part of stagflation. The other part of it, high unemployment, we're still sitting at a 4.3% unemployment rate and we still have pretty resilient economic growth on a real basis, meaning adjusted for inflation. So that stag portion is missing from the classic stagflation definition. Now the big question as we move forward is whether or not the current higher inflation that we are seeing will start to weigh on growth in the form of demand destruction. So I think the biggest macro story right now is energy prices. This time we have 12% of the world's crude oil off the market. Essentially twice as much as we saw during the twin oil crises of the 1970s. And so far oil prices are up by only 100%. So the upside potential is potentially very very large if the conflict drags out. >> The biggest structural difference between today and the 1970s energy shock was just how sensitive the US economy was to energy prices back in the 1970s. The key portion that we can look at is that energy costs as a percentage of total spending was far higher in the 1970s. Because of all of the pushes to efficiency over the course of the last 50 plus years, we've actually seen a US economy that relies less on energy as an input for final goods. >> After having gone through co, we've all learned that we can work from home and that we can do virtual online meetings, things that were not possible back in the 1970s. So it could be that the oil market doesn't respond with such an extreme price move this time as it did in the past. >> So the big thing to watch with oil markets is not necessarily what the paper price of WTI or Brent crude does. What really matters for the broader market is the price of diesel and gasoline. That's the thing that will impact corporate earnings. That's the thing that will impact the path forward for the Fed. And of course that is the thing that will impact overall consumers.
