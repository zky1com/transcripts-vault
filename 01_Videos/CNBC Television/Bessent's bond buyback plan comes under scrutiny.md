Scott Bessent said the US Treasury will stick to its planned auctions of long-dated bonds even after he announced an operation that would at least double buybacks of those same securities to at least $4 billion per operation. That expanded buyback program is expected to begin on September 10th for 10 and 20-year durations, according to Reuters. But that's just a drop in the ocean compared to the nearly $740 billion in debt the Treasury expects to offer to investors this quarter. It also isn't clear how Bessent plans to pay for the move. The Treasury isn't the Fed, it can't print its own money. One way would be to borrow at shorter durations, and the other would be to use the Treasury's own funds, as sources have told CNBC. Bessent has built the Treasury's general account to nearly a trillion dollars. He could run that down a little with bond purchases and still have some time to replenish it before any potential debt ceiling impasse down the line. Of course, that would entail selling even more bonds. And the move is coming under scrutiny. Stanley Druckenmiller penning an op-ed in the Wall Street Journal warning that rising rates are a sign of trouble ahead and that artificially suppressing it only heightens the danger. In the article, Bessent's former mentor writes that governments defending prices against fundamentals always lose, saying the US shouldn't put itself on the wrong side of that trade. Meanwhile, a note out of Citadel has warned that tighter monetary policy may be in order as a solution to the debt problem is not repeated intervention, but harder choices on fiscal policy and central banks willing to get ahead of inflation. Also signaling uncertainty, the gold price, which is on course for its best month this century. And the unease is spreading across the Atlantic. Germany's finance minister Lars Klingbeil's laying the blame for rising yields firmly at America's door, telling reporters that they are a consequence of global uncertainty brought about by the war that Donald Trump started in Iran. German borrowing costs are currently trading around a 15-year high.
