We talk a lot about the massive bet investors are making on data centers, but one thing that seems to be left out of the conversation recently is the risk from climate change. But a new study is taking that on, showing that 79% of data centers globally face acute hazards from climate change, including flooding, extreme winds, and wildfires that can disrupt operations, increase downtime, and drive insurance and repair costs. This is all from First Street, a climate risk analytics firm. They looked at 97 global data centers for the report. First Street CEO Matthew Eie said, "Most underwriting for real assets still uses historical data, but the climate is no longer behaving the way the historical record would predict. As heat, drought, and water stress increase, outdated models simply don't offer a complete view of risk anymore." The study found that just over half of all data centers globally are in markets exposed to chronic climate stress such as extreme heat and drought that hit energy efficiency and increased costs. While risk can be heightened by exposure to singular climate driven events such as strong hurricanes, it's the chronic effects of climate change that can cause the most damage both physically and financially. Backward-looking climate models, some from the US government agencies, are therefore underestimating the risk, especially in the case of heavier rainfall. The danger, according to the study's findings, is that investors may be looking at traditional metrics when developing data centers and not taking into account that climate could impact long-term operating conditions. Data centers are typically expected to be in operation for 20 to 30 years. Now, some areas are more prone to climate risk than others. The study found that the Asia-Pacific region has 89% of its data center capacity at risk, the highest percentage of any geographic area. That compares with 50% exposure in the Americas and 46% in Europe, the Middle East, and Africa. Some of the study's fastest growing markets are also the most exposed, the study found, and that includes Northern Virginia right here in the US.
